The World Remembers · the closing exhibit · the evidence for Jesus of Nazareth

Following the Seed Promise

The book has followed the world's memory from the waters of the deep to the witnesses who carried it down. It began in a garden with a promise: the woman's seed would crush the serpent's head (Genesis 3:15). It closes here, following that promise to Jesus of Nazareth through the verifiable record: ancient texts, manuscripts, inscriptions, excavated objects, and measurements, each cited so any reader can check it.

§1How to read this file

Every exhibit gives what the source says, its date, and where it can be checked. Where a critic objects, the exhibit sets out the case, the objection with its own evidence, and the answer from the sources. Measured data that cuts against a claim is stated plainly, once. Nothing is hidden and nothing is softened. No appeal to "most scholars" is used on either side; the sources do the talking.

  • Physical object something excavated or held in a collection
  • Ancient text a writing with a manuscript tradition
  • Measurement lab dating, astronomy, statistics
  • Contested an objection exists; it is stated and answered
  • Refuted shown false; listed so it is not used
  • New since 1998 found or published after the original book

Translations. Every English quotation is from a named translation: NASB 1995 for Scripture, Whiston for Josephus, Church & Brodribb for Tacitus, Melmoth/Bosanquet for Pliny, Rolfe (1914) for Suetonius, the Fowlers for Lucian, Crombie (ANF) for Origen, Herford for the Talmud, and the Ante-Nicene Fathers for the church fathers. Key original-language words are given beside them.

§3Witnesses outside the church

These are Roman, Jewish, and pagan writers. Most were indifferent or openly hostile to Christians. They are listed in order of strength of attestation.

H-01

Tacitus, Annals 15.44

Ancient textHostile
Author, date
Cornelius Tacitus, Roman senator and governor of Asia, writing about AD 115–120 about the fire of Rome in AD 64.
Says
"…called Christians by the populace. Christus, from whom the name had its origin, suffered the extreme penalty during the reign of Tiberius at the hands of one of our procurators, Pontius Pilatus, and a most mischievous superstition, thus checked for the moment, again broke out not only in Judæa, the first source of the evil, but even in Rome…" (Church & Brodribb, 1876)auctor nominis eius Christus Tiberio imperitante per procuratorem Pontium Pilatum supplicio adfectus erat
Manuscript
The only witness is the Second Medicean, Laur. Plut. 68.2, 11th c., from Monte Cassino. The word Chrestianos was corrected to Christianos by erasing the e (H. Fuchs, Vigiliae Christianae 4, 1950; UV photograph 2008). Sulpicius Severus (Chronica 2.29, about AD 400) paraphrases the passage.
Weighed
The caseIt is in the only manuscript. The style is Tacitus's, and the tone is contemptuous: exitiabilis superstitio, "deadly superstition." No Christian would have written that about his own faith.
ObjectionR. Carrier, Vigiliae Christianae 68 (2014) 264–283: the Christus sentence was inserted. B. Shaw, JRS 105 (2015) 73–100: the persecution of 64 is a later construct.
AnswerNo manuscript lacks the sentence; the objection is conjecture against the only witness. The first hand wrote Chrestianos, the crowd's name for them, which Tacitus sets against the correct Christus. That is a Roman writer's distinction, not a Christian's. C.P. Jones answered Shaw in NTS 63 (2017) 146–152.

H-02

Josephus, Antiquities 18.63–64: the Testimonium Flavianum

Ancient textObjection answeredMajor studies 2024–25
Author, date
Flavius Josephus, Jewish priest, commander in the revolt, later a client of the Flavian emperors. Written about AD 93–94.
Says
"Now there was about this time Jesus, a wise man, if it be lawful to call him a man; for he was a doer of wonderful works… He was [the] Christ. And when Pilate, at the suggestion of the principal men amongst us, had condemned him to the cross, those that loved him at the first did not forsake him; for he appeared to them alive again the third day… And the tribe of Christians, so named from him, are not extinct at this day." (Whiston)σοφὸς ἀνήρ, εἴγε ἄνδρα αὐτὸν λέγειν χρή … παραδόξων ἔργων ποιητής … ὁ χριστὸς οὗτος ἦν
Witnesses
Every Greek manuscript has it; the oldest is Ambrosianus 370, 11th c. About 170 Latin manuscripts have it. Eusebius is the first to quote it (HE 1.11.7–8, about 324). Origen (about 248) says Josephus "did not believe in Jesus as the Christ" (Contra Celsum 1.47). Jerome's Latin reads "he was believed to be the Christ." The 10th-c. Arabic of Agapius (S. Pines, 1971) and the 12th-c. Syriac of Michael the Syrian are milder in wording.
Weighed
The caseT.C. Schmidt, Josephus and Jesus: New Evidence for the One Called Christ (Oxford UP, 2025, open access): the passage's style matches Josephus by stylometry; early readers took it as neutral or mildly negative; Josephus knew people connected to the trial. Antiquities 20.200 (H-03) refers to "Jesus, who was called Christ" as someone the reader has already met. Every Greek and Latin manuscript contains the passage. Steve Mason, Irish Theological Quarterly 91.1 (2026) 74–98: Schmidt's case for authenticity "must be taken seriously"; Mason rejects only Schmidt's proposed informants.
ObjectionK. Olson (CBQ 61, 1999; Harvard/CHS 2013): the vocabulary matches Eusebius's Demonstratio 3.5. C. Hansen, New England Classical Journal 51.2 (2024) 50–75: every surviving version traces back to Eusebius. Origen says Josephus "did not believe in Jesus as the Christ." D.R. Schwartz, Brill commentary on Antiquities 18–20 (2024): "total authenticity seems to be ruled out."
AnswerOrigen's remark fits a neutral original with a few Christian phrases added. The milder Arabic and Syriac versions read that way (A. Whealey, NTS 54, 2008, 573–590). Strip those phrases and a notice remains of a wise man and wonder-worker, condemned by Pilate, whose followers did not abandon him. 20.200 still names him independently.

H-03

Josephus, Antiquities 20.200: "the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ"

Ancient text
Says
The high priest Ananus, between governors in AD 62, convened the Sanhedrin
"and brought before them the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ, whose name was James, and some others… he delivered them to be stoned." (Whiston)τὸν ἀδελφὸν Ἰησοῦ τοῦ λεγομένου Χριστοῦ, Ἰάκωβος ὄνομα αὐτῷ
Witnesses
Every Greek manuscript has it. Origen knew it (Comm. Matt. 10.17; Contra Celsum 1.47) about 70 years before Eusebius quoted it (HE 2.23.21–24).
Weighed
The case"Who was called Christ" is the phrase of a non-believer, not a confession. The James named here matches Paul's "James the Lord's brother" (Gal 1:19, C-02), and Hegesippus records the same stoning (Eusebius HE 2.23).
ObjectionR. Carrier, JECS 20 (2012): a marginal note slipped in, and this James is the brother of Jesus son of Damneus (20.203). C. Hansen, Studies of Biblical Interest 2 (2025) 1–19: Eusebius inserted it.
AnswerEvery Greek manuscript has the phrase, and Origen read it about 70 years before Eusebius. At 20.203 Josephus identifies the other man as "son of Damneus." Here he identifies the brother through a Jesus "called Christ," which is a different identifier for a different man.
H-04

Josephus, Antiquities 18.116–119: John the Baptist

Ancient text
Says
"…John, that was called the Baptist: for Herod slew him, who was a good man…" (Whiston)
Josephus says Herod Antipas killed him at Machaerus because he feared John's influence.
Fixes
The Gospels' forerunner and his executioner (Mark 6:14–29) appear in a Jewish historian, with details that differ from the Gospel account.
Weighed
The caseIt is in all manuscripts and Origen cites it (Contra Celsum 1.47).
ObjectionR. Nir, JSHJ 10 (2012) 32–62: the baptism theology fits Christian practice, and the title "the Baptist" appears nowhere else in Josephus.
AnswerJosephus gives a different reason for the execution than the Gospels do: Herod's fear of an uprising, not John's rebuke of his marriage. A Christian forger would have copied the Gospel account.

Suetonius, Claudius 25.4 and Nero 16.2

Ancient textObjection answered
Says
"Since the Jews constantly made disturbances at the instigation of Chrestus, he expelled them from Rome." (Rolfe 1914)Iudaeos impulsore Chresto assidue tumultuantis Roma expulit
"Punishment was inflicted on the Christians, a class of men given to a new and mischievous superstition." (Nero 16.2)
Cross-check

Weighed
The case"Chrestus" was the common pagan spelling of "Christus." Tacitus's manuscript also first wrote "Chrestianos" (H-01). Acts 18:2 ties the same expulsion to Aquila and Priscilla, Jewish Christians. Orosius read the name as Christ.
ObjectionChrestus was a common slave name, and Suetonius writes as if the man were in Rome.
AnswerNo Roman Jewish agitator named Chrestus is known from any other source. A Roman writing 70 years later about Jews quarrelling over "Chrestus" is describing a dispute over Christ, as the Acts parallel shows.

Mara bar Serapion: "their wise king"

Ancient textUnnamed; date discussed
Says
"Or the Jews by the murder of their Wise King, seeing that from that very time their kingdom was driven away from them?" (ANF vol. 8, Cureton)

Thallus, quoted by Julius Africanus

Ancient textQuoted by Africanus
Says
"This darkness Thallus, in the third book of his History, calls, as appears to me without reason, an eclipse of the sun." (Africanus, ANF vol. 6)
This is Africanus reporting Thallus, not Thallus's own words.
Chain
Thallus (date unknown; first mentioned by Theophilus, To Autolycus 3.29, about AD 180) → Julius Africanus, Chronographiae (about 221) → George Syncellus (about 810).
The case
A pagan historian knew the darkness at the crucifixion as an event needing explanation, and explained it as an eclipse. An eclipse is impossible at the Passover full moon (T-04), as Africanus pointed out. Thallus's own date is unknown (R. Carrier, JGRChJ 8, 2011–12, 185–191).

H-11

Celsus, The True Word, quoted in Origen's Contra Celsum

Ancient textHostile
Says
Jesus, "having hired himself out as a servant in Egypt on account of his poverty, and having there acquired some miraculous powers … returned to his own country … and by means of these proclaimed himself a God" (1.28). His mother was said to have conceived by "a certain soldier named Panthera" (1.32). He worked his wonders "by help of magic" (1.38, Origen's summary). On the resurrection: "Who beheld this? A half-frantic woman, as you state, and some other one, perhaps, of those who were engaged in the same system of delusion" (2.55). (Crombie, ANF vol. 4)
Date
Celsus wrote about AD 175–180. Origen answered him point by point about 248, quoting him at length.
What a hostile witness grants
Celsus, the movement's most thorough 2nd-century opponent, does not deny that Jesus lived, that he did wonders, that his birth was out of the ordinary, that he was crucified, or that his followers reported seeing him risen. He explains each one away: magic learned in Egypt, illegitimacy, delusion. At 1.68 he grants the works for the sake of argument and asks why they prove divinity rather than sorcery. The one thing he calls invented is the earthquake and darkness (2.59).

H-12

Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 43a

Ancient textHostileObjection answered
Says
"On the eve of Pesah they hung Jeshu [the Nazarene]. And the crier went forth before him forty days (saying), '[Jeshu the Nazarene] goeth forth to be stoned, because he hath practised magic and deceived and led astray Israel.'" (R.T. Herford, 1903)
The words in brackets come from manuscripts. Herford notes "the whole of this passage is expunged from the later editions."
Manuscripts
Florence codex (1177). Munich Cod. hebr. 95 (1342/43, fol. 342r) shows the censor's erasures. The passage was removed from printed editions after the Basel Talmud (1578–81). It is a baraita, a tradition the Talmud attributes to the period of the Tannaim (before about AD 200).
Weighed
The caseThe uncensored manuscripts read "Yeshu the Nazarene." The passage has the Passover-eve execution and the charge of sorcery (compare Mark 3:22). The same passage names five disciples, the first of them Mattai. D. Instone-Brewer, Tyndale Bulletin 62.2 (2011); P. Schäfer, Jesus in the Talmud (Princeton UP, 2007).
ObjectionJ. Maier (1978): these passages were not originally about Jesus. The 40-day herald conflicts with the Gospels.
AnswerChurch censors cut this passage from printed editions after 1578 because it was read as being about Jesus. The herald is the polemic's own addition: it makes the trial look fair. The core is execution on Passover eve, for "sorcery" and leading Israel astray. That is a hostile report of the same events.

H-13

Roman records claimed but not extant: the "Acts of Pilate"

Claim only
Says
Justin, 1 Apology 35: "And that these things did happen, you can ascertain from the Acts of Pontius Pilate." Also 48: "And that He did those things, you can learn from the Acts of Pontius Pilate." Tertullian, Apology 5: Tiberius, "having himself received intelligence from Palestine … brought the matter before the senate"; Apology 21: Pilate "sent word of Him to the reigning Cæsar."

§4The earliest Christian witnesses

Paul's letters are the earliest datable Christian writing. One inscription fixes his clock: Claudius's letter at Delphi puts Gallio in office as proconsul of Achaia in AD 51–52 (some argue 52–53), and Acts 18:12 has Paul before Gallio in Corinth (exhibit L-02). Working outward from that anchor, the letters below were written within roughly 20 to 30 years of the crucifixion. Scripture is quoted from the NASB 1995, with the Greek where a word carries weight.

C-01

1 Corinthians 15:3–8: the handed-down creed

Ancient text
Says
"For I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received, that Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures, and that He was buried, and that He was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures, and that He appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve. After that He appeared to more than five hundred brethren at one time, most of whom remain until now, but some have fallen asleep; then He appeared to James, then to all the apostles; and last of all, as to one untimely born, He appeared to me also."παρέδωκα γὰρ ὑμῖν ἐν πρώτοις, ὃ καὶ παρέλαβον: "I delivered … which I also received"
Date
The letter was written from Ephesus (1 Cor 16:8), after Paul's Corinth stay of about AD 50–52, so about AD 53–55. Paul says he passed the content on when he founded the church (about 50). He says he had "received" it before that.
What it fixes
It names the witnesses: Cephas (Peter), the Twelve, more than 500 at once, James, all the apostles, and Paul. It says most of the 500 were still alive when he wrote. "Received / delivered" (παραλαμβάνω / παραδίδωμι) are the words used for passing on a fixed tradition. The same pair opens 1 Cor 11:23.
The case
Two of the named witnesses, Cephas and James, are the two men Paul spent time with in Jerusalem three years after his conversion (Gal 1:18–19, C-02). The resurrection proclamation was being handed on in fixed form within a few years of the cross, by men who said they saw him.
C-02

Galatians 1:18–19 and 2:1–9: Paul meets Peter and "James the Lord's brother"

Ancient text
Says
"Then three years later I went up to Jerusalem to become acquainted with Cephas, and stayed with him fifteen days. But I did not see any other of the apostles except James, the Lord’s brother."ἱστορῆσαι Κηφᾶν: "to visit / inquire of Cephas"

Date
Galatians is about AD 48–55, depending on its destination. The first visit is about AD 35–37.
What it fixes
A writer we can date says in the first person that he met Jesus's chief disciple and a man he calls Jesus's brother. Josephus names the same brother independently (H-03).
C-03

Other details in Paul's letters

Ancient text
Says
  • Last Supper, betrayal at night: "the Lord Jesus in the night in which He was betrayed took bread…" (1 Cor 11:23–26; again "received … delivered").
  • Davidic descent: "born of a descendant of David according to the flesh, who was declared the Son of God with power by the resurrection from the dead" (Rom 1:3–4).

Date

C-04

The four Gospels and Acts

Ancient text
What
Four narrative accounts of Jesus's life, death and reported resurrection, plus Acts, a sequel that runs to Paul's house arrest in Rome.
Dating
The caseActs ends with Paul alive in Rome (about AD 62). It never mentions Paul's death, James's death (AD 62, H-03), Nero's persecution (64), or the fall of Jerusalem and the Temple (70). So Acts was written before about 62, Luke before Acts (Acts 1:1), and Mark before Luke. J.A.T. Robinson, Redating the New Testament (1976).
ObjectionMark about 65–75, Matthew and Luke about 80–90, John about 90–100, because Mark 13 and Luke 21:20 describe the fall of Jerusalem.
AnswerThat dating assumes Jesus could not have foretold the city's fall. It is a premise, not a manuscript or archaeological datum. Acts' silence on the events of 62–70 is a datum.
Earliest attributions
Papias (about 110–130, in Eusebius HE 3.39.15–16): "Mark, having become the interpreter of Peter," wrote accurately but not in order; "Matthew wrote the oracles in the Hebrew language." Irenaeus (about 180, Against Heresies 3.1.1): Matthew wrote among the Hebrews "while Peter and Paul were preaching at Rome"; Mark was Peter's interpreter; Luke was Paul's companion; John published at Ephesus. Sources: Eusebius 3 · Irenaeus 3.1
Checks against the record
See the Luke–Acts inscriptions (§7), the names test (T-06), and the places in §6.

Seven letters, written on the road to his execution in Rome. Dating positions are about 110 (Eusebius) or the 140s (T.D. Barnes, ExpTim 120, 2008). Trallians 9: "of Mary … truly born, and ate and drank … truly persecuted under Pontius Pilate … truly crucified … truly raised from the dead." Smyrnaeans 1: "under Pontius Pilate and Herod the tetrarch, nailed [to the cross] for us in His flesh"; 3: "Lay hold, handle Me, and see that I am not an incorporeal spirit." text
Polycarp
To the Philippians, about 110–140. Irenaeus says he saw Polycarp "in my early youth" and that Polycarp was "instructed by apostles" (AH 3.3.4). The direct link between Polycarp and John is in Irenaeus's letter to Florinus (Eusebius HE 5.20.6). text
Quadratus
Apology to Hadrian (about AD 125), quoted by Eusebius HE 4.3.2: those healed and raised by the Saviour were still around after he left, and some survived "to our day." text
Hegesippus
About 170, quoted by Eusebius. James's death by stoning and clubbing (HE 2.23). The grandsons of Jude, "the Lord's brother according to the flesh," were brought before Domitian and showed their farmers' hands (HE 3.20).
Justin Martyr
About 150–160. On Sunday "the memoirs of the apostles … which are called Gospels" are read (1 Apology 66–67). Dialogue 108 reports what the Jewish authorities were saying: "his disciples stole him by night from the tomb." text
Didache
A church manual. Dating positions are about 50–70 (J.-P. Audet, 1958) or about 110–120 (K. Niederwimmer, 1998). It is a composite document.

Crucifixion as it was practiced

A-06

Yehohanan: the nailed heel, Jerusalem

Physical object
Found
1968, Giv'at ha-Mivtar, Jerusalem, excavated by V. Tzaferis. A right heel bone with an iron nail about 11.5 cm long still in it, and wood under the nail head. Ossuary inscription: יהוחנן בן חגקול "Yehohanan son of Hagqol." A replica is shown in the Israel Museum.
Published

A-07

Fenstanton, England, and Gavello, Italy: two more crucified men

Physical objectPublished 2018–21
Fenstanton

If this were Jesus's ossuary, it would be evidence that he existed and against a bodily resurrection. That is why both sides fight over it.

§12Do not use: refuted claims and press errors

Using a bad exhibit sinks a good case. These items still circulate and should not be cited, on either side.

R-01

The "Gospel of Jesus' Wife" papyrus

Refuted2012–2016
Claim
A Coptic fragment reading "Jesus said to them, 'My wife…'." K. King, HTR 107 (2014).
Refuted
A. Sabar, The Atlantic (July/Aug 2016), traced it to its owner, Walter Fritz, and showed the provenance was false. Its handwriting matches a forged John fragment from the same source. It copies a typo from an online Gospel of Thomas edition posted in 2002. King said the evidence "tips the balance toward forgery."
R-02

"First-century Mark"

Refuted2012–2018
Claim
Announced in 2012 as a 1st-century fragment of Mark, with Dirk Obbink as the source.
Result
Published in 2018 as P137 (P.Oxy. LXXXIII 5345), dated later 2nd to earlier 3rd century (M-03). Obbink was later caught up in a separate scandal over the sale of Oxyrhynchus papyri.
R-03

"Olive trees from AD 33 under the Holy Sepulchre"

Cite accurately2025
Claim
Headlines in 2025 said olive trees under the church had been dated to AD 33.
Actual finding
The garden is real and earlier than Hadrian (A-18). It is dated by its layering; no radiocarbon date has been published. Cite the garden and the quarry tombs, not a year.
R-04

"1 in 600": the Talpiot odds

Refuted as stated
Facts
A. Feuerverger, Annals of Applied Statistics 2 (2008) 3–54. The headline odds depended on assumptions supplied by the filmmakers. The decisive one was reading "Mariamenou" as Mary Magdalene, which moved the odds from about 1 in 3 to about 1 in 80. R. Ingermanson, in the same issue (pp. 84–90), lists six faulty assumptions and estimates under 2%.
R-05

Modern forgeries still passed around: the Archko Volume and the Letter of Lentulus

Refuted
Archko Volume
Published by W.D. Mahan in the 1880s as reports from Pilate and the Sanhedrin. Its story of the Magi was copied word for word from Lew Wallace's novel Ben-Hur, misprints included. E.J. Goodspeed, Strange New Gospels (1931) and Modern Apocrypha (1956). Goodspeed text
Lentulus
A physical description of Jesus attributed to a "Publius Lentulus," governor of Judea, writing to the Roman Senate. No such governor existed, and the letter first appears in medieval Latin manuscripts.
R-06

Correction
Syriac Matthew
The 2023 find is a 6th-century copy of a translation made as early as the 3rd century. It is not a 3rd-century manuscript (M-04).

§13Writings outside the canon: read with caution

These texts are kept apart from the main record. Most are later than the New Testament, and several teach doctrines at odds with the apostolic writings. Their manuscripts are listed as finds. Their teaching is summarized only as far as needed, and nothing here is used as evidence elsewhere in the file.

G-01

Gospel of Thomas

Non-canonicalCaution
Manuscripts
Coptic: Nag Hammadi Codex II (found 1945, 4th c.). Greek fragments: P.Oxy. 1, 654, 655 (about AD 200–250).
Content
114 sayings with no narrative of death or resurrection. Some parallel the Gospels; others reflect later Gnostic teaching. Dates proposed run from the mid-1st to mid-2nd century.
G-02

Gospel of Judas

Non-canonicalCautionPublished 2006
Facts
Codex Tchacos (Coptic, 3rd–4th c.), published in 2006. Irenaeus already names a sect's "Gospel of Judas" about AD 180 (Against Heresies 1.31.1). Irenaeus attributes it to the sect later called Cainites. It is not an independent source for the life of Jesus.
G-03

Infancy Gospel of Thomas: earliest copy found in 2024

Non-canonicalPublished 2024
Facts
P.Hamb.Graec. 1011, Hamburg State and University Library, 4th–5th c., 13 lines. L. Berkes & G. Nocchi Macedo, ZPE 229 (2024). The previous earliest Greek copy was 11th c. It contains legends of Jesus's boyhood with no claim to eyewitness origin. press release
G-04

P.Oxy. 5575: a sayings papyrus

Non-canonicalPublished 2023
Facts
Published in P.Oxy. LXXXVII (2023). It mixes sayings parallel to Matthew and Luke with Thomas-type sayings, and is not registered as a New Testament manuscript.
